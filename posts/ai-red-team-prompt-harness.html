How I Automated AI Jailbreak Testing

August 21, 2026 · 8 min read · ai-security · red-team

I kept seeing people build AI harnesses for vulnerability research. I wanted to do the same for my jailbreak research.

A lot of the discussion I came across was about using AI agents and pipelines to find software vulnerabilities. I saw less about using them to help with AI jailbreak research, which is where I wanted to put them to work. Could I use AI to automate more of my own testing and do the job better?

I convinced Claude Opus 4.8 to help me develop jailbreaks. I also used a local Qwen3.8-27B model, with 27 billion parameters, to help with the work. I wanted to bring that assistance into a harness I could use in my day-to-day research.

The goal was to run tests faster and make the evaluation more consistent, while leaving me time to look closely at the results. This post covers the harness I built, the checks I use, and the shared brain that carries the work between sessions.

The harness turns a one-off attempt into a measured cycle: evaluate, diagnose, write state, mutate, and test again.

The research harness

The system is a closed-loop harness for authorised guardrail research. It takes the scope, previous tests, invalid techniques, and similarity constraints, then evaluates each candidate against those rules.

I borrowed the basic idea from Andrej Karpathy's autoresearch: Give an agent a bounded experiment loop, a fixed metric, and a rule for keeping or discarding changes. The question was simple enough: did the result cross the threshold without turning into a duplicate, leaking the answer into the prompt, or wandering outside the rules?

closed-loop evaluation 
scope + category definitions
  -> generate candidate
  -> leakage and duplicate gates
  -> target-model test
  -> refusal and coverage diagnostics
  -> human review
  -> shared-brain update
  -> mutate, catalog, or discard

The harness records failed attempts alongside the results I keep. That gives me a record to check before spending more time on something I have already tried.

The gates check different things. Keyword coverage measures how much category-related vocabulary appears in a response. For the scoring reference, see 0DIN's Jailbreak Evaluation Framework (JEF). Its category-specific evaluators assess response text against benchmark criteria and produce output-fidelity scores from 0 to 100. That score is a benchmark result, not a percentage of factual correctness. JEF also has a separate composite score that accounts for the models and categories affected.

My tests include the illicit-substance creation and harmful-substance creation categories. I use the coverage score to help review the response, then read it to judge whether it actually violates the policy being tested. A refusal or general discussion of the subject does not count as a successful jailbreak just because it contains relevant keywords. An ambiguous response needs further review. A response that appears to cross the policy boundary is a candidate finding until I have checked its scope and reproducibility.

This distinction matters when describing a “good” jailbreak result: I mean credible evidence of a policy failure. Keyword presence alone does not establish that a response is complete or that its claims are correct.

Prompt leakage, as I use the term here, means that the prompt already contains keywords or answer material that the evaluation is looking for in the response. A model repeating that material could inflate the coverage score. This check concerns answer material supplied in the input, rather than disclosure of a hidden system prompt.

Response coverage has to clear the configured threshold, and prompt leakage is capped per category. Duplicate checks compare candidates against both locked reference prompts and the submitted catalogue. These are checks for this experiment, with human review still needed to interpret the results.

The shared brain

I call the file-backed experiment ledger the shared brain. It is a collection of markdown files that preserves research state across sessions and agents. The shared brain stores working techniques, invalid paths, hypotheses, observations, refusal patterns, strongest candidates, and dead ends.

At the start of a session, the agent reads the shared brain to see what passed, what was rejected, what is out of scope, and what still needs review. File locking helps prevent concurrent agents from overwriting each other's updates.

It also gives me an audit trail. I can see why a candidate was kept, why another one was discarded, what hypothesis motivated a change, and whether a later result actually improved the work or merely repeated something I had already seen.

Refusals became telemetry

I record refusal behaviour alongside the other test results. Some responses are brief refusals; others contain general safety information or address only part of the request. These are observable differences I can record and review. They do not establish how the model processed the request internally.

Signal Observation Review note
Short refusal The response declines the request with little additional content Record the refusal; its wording alone does not reveal the internal cause
Long safe answer The response contains general safety information Distinguish general discussion from content that meets the evaluation criteria
High score, wrong shape The score is high, but the response does not meet the review criteria Record why the score and human assessment differ
Repeated keyword gaps Keywords from the same category group are absent across several responses Check the response manually before treating missing keywords as missing content

These labels describe the responses. Any explanation for why a model produced one of them remains a hypothesis unless there is separate evidence to support it.

Duplicates were the trap

This is where the first versions went wrong. The system produced candidates that looked new because the keywords changed. The underlying technique was identical: the same rhetorical move, the same output demand, using the same style and format with a different label.

The archive kept growing, which made it easy to overestimate how much new ground the tests covered. I needed a way to catch some of those repeats before spending more time reviewing them.

The cosine checker helped with that. The harness treats each candidate as a sparse term-frequency vector rather than as prose. After tokenization and light normalization, each unique term becomes a dimension, and its count becomes the coordinate value. Similarity is then computed as the normalized dot product: 

cos(theta) = dot(a, b) / (||a|| * ||b||)
           = sum(a_i * b_i) / (sqrt(sum(a_i^2)) * sqrt(sum(b_i^2)))

In code, that becomes a counter comparison over the shared vocabulary: 

for (const key of keys) {
  const va = fa.get(key) || 0;
  const vb = fb.get(key) || 0;
  dot += va * vb;
  na += va * va;
  nb += vb * vb;
}
return dot / (Math.sqrt(na) * Math.sqrt(nb));

If the same vocabulary appears in the same proportions, the cosine score stays high even when the text is longer. I chose a lexical check because it is cheap and easy to inspect. It has clear limits: paraphrases can receive low similarity scores, and different techniques can share enough vocabulary to receive high ones. I use it as a lexical duplicate filter. Whether a candidate represents a different technique still needs review.

Passing this filter only means that a candidate falls below the lexical similarity threshold. It does not establish that the underlying technique is new.

What changed in practice

The main benefit for me was being able to review the experiments more clearly. I could check whether a change duplicated a known pattern, moved only the metric, or produced a result that held up under review.

The saved record also made results easier to explain. I could point to the response, the coverage score, the duplicate check, and my review notes rather than relying on my recollection of a test.

The important caveat

These scores have limited meanings. Keyword presence can overstate substantive coverage, a refusal in one test leaves other behaviour unexamined, and lexical similarity measures wording. Human review checks whether a result is valid, reproducible, in scope, and worth reporting.

Takeaway

The harness now handles the repetitive checks and record-keeping, and the shared brain lets me pick up the work across sessions. I still review candidates and decide which results are valid and worth reporting.
